Student engagement detection · plain language

How the whole thing actually works

A computer watches a ten-second video of someone in a lecture and guesses how much attention they are paying, on a scale of 0 to 3. This page walks the whole machine, one station at a time, in the simplest words that are still true.

The one-sentence version

Chop the video into pictures, throw away the bad pictures, draw dots on the face in the good ones, let two different helpers describe what they see, and have a small brain turn that description into a number.

The clever part — the bit that is supposed to be new — is the throwing away. Most systems treat every picture as equally trustworthy. This one gives each picture a score first, bins the worst, and then keeps carrying that score forward so the rest of the machine knows which pictures to lean on.

01

Take pictures, bin the bad ones

A video is just a lot of pictures shown quickly. We take 5 pictures every second, so a ten-second clip gives about 50.

Some pictures are no good. The person moved and went blurry. They turned to look out of the window. They blinked. Or the computer briefly lost the face entirely. If you feed those to the rest of the machine, it learns from mush.

So every picture gets a score out of 10, from five separate checks.

Two extra jobs at this station

Following the right person. These videos were filmed in shared rooms, so other people wander through the background. If we just grabbed "the clearest face" in every picture, we would sometimes grab a stranger. So we lock onto whoever we picked first and follow them.

Straightening every face. We rotate and zoom each face so the eyes always land in exactly the same two spots — like lining up passport photos. Now every picture is comparable to every other picture.

In grown-up words Sample at 5 fps, detect faces with BlazeFace, track the primary subject across frames, align on the eye line to a 224×224 crop, then compute the Motion Reliability Score from blur (variance of Laplacian), detector confidence, head-pose deviation, eye-aspect-ratio, and inter-frame optical flow. Discard below mrs_threshold.
02

Draw dots on the face

On every picture we kept, we place 478 tiny dots on the face — around the eyes, the eyebrows, the mouth, the nose, along the jaw, and even a ring of dots on the coloured part of each eye.

Once you have the dots, you can measure things with them, the way you would with a ruler:

  • How far apart are the top and bottom eyelids? That tells you about blinking.
  • Which way is the nose pointing? That tells you where the head is turned.
  • Where is the iris sitting inside the eye? That tells you where they are looking.
  • How much is the mouth open? Are the eyebrows raised?

Every measurement is divided by the distance between the eyes first. That way it does not matter whether someone sat close to the camera or far away — the numbers still mean the same thing.

In grown-up words MediaPipe Face Mesh gives 468 landmarks plus 10 iris-refinement points. Head pose comes from the facial transformation matrix, cross-checked with solvePnP. All geometric descriptors are normalised by inter-ocular distance for scale invariance.
03

The reader that flips through the pictures

Now imagine the kept pictures stacked into a flipbook. We hand that flipbook to a reader called Mamba.

Mamba flips through one page at a time and keeps a memory of what it has seen so far — the way you hold the plot of a story in your head as you read. Each new page nudges that memory a bit.

It does two things worth knowing about. First, it reads the flipbook both ways — front to back, then back to front — because in a video what happens next often explains what happened before. Second, and this is the idea the project is built around: it reads the low-scoring pages more gently. A page we were unsure about is allowed to change Mamba's memory less than a page we trust.

In grown-up words A bidirectional Vision Mamba (S6 selective state-space model). Each 112×112 crop becomes 49 patch tokens; two blocks scan the patch grid, two more scan across time. The reliability score scales the SSM timestep dt, so a low-reliability frame moves the hidden state less — plus the temporal pooling is reliability-weighted.
04

Two helpers and a judge

Two different helpers now describe the same clip, in completely different ways.

  • Helper A — the picture reader. Mamba, from the last station. It looked at the actual faces and formed its own impression. Nobody told it what to look for.
  • Helper B — the ruler. This one ignores the pictures and only reads the dots. It reports 32 measurements per picture: how much the head wobbled, how often they blinked, where the eyes pointed, how much the mouth moved.

Helper B is easier to trust, because you can read its answer out loud: "the head barely moved, they blinked 12 times a minute, they looked slightly down." Helper A's answer is just 128 numbers that mean nothing to a human.

Then a judge decides, for this particular clip, how much to listen to each helper, and blends them.

In grown-up words Both streams project to a common width; a per-channel sigmoid gate interpolates g·mamba + (1−g)·geometric, with the elementwise product concatenated so information survives gate saturation. Measured gate mean: 0.493, range 0.350–0.625.
05

The small brain that answers

The blended description goes into a small brain — just a few layers of arithmetic — which turns it into one number: 0, 1, 2 or 3.

It is deliberately small. We only have 36 videos to learn from, and a big brain given 36 examples does not learn the pattern, it memorises the 36 answers and then fails on anything new.

In grown-up words A two-layer MLP (128 → 64) with LayerNorm, GELU and dropout, trained with AdamW, cosine schedule, class-weighted cross-entropy and early stopping on validation macro-F1.
06

Take a piece out and see if it still works

How do you know each part is pulling its weight? You take it out and see whether the answers get worse. Like removing one ingredient from a recipe to find out what it was doing.

We built seven versions, each missing exactly one piece, and compared them. This is where the surprises came from.

In grown-up words Ablation study. All seven variants share one seed, one data cache and one training schedule, so any difference is attributable to the removed component.
07

Ask it why

Finally we ask the machine: which measurements made you say that? It answers in percentages, and the answer was:

  • 41% — face movement (mouth, eyebrows, how much the face changed)
  • 23% — head movement
  • 19% — where the eyes were looking
  • 12% — blinking
  • 4.5% — everything the picture reader contributed

That last line is the uncomfortable one. The most sophisticated part of the whole system is contributing almost nothing.

In grown-up words SHAP over named behavioural features rather than raw embedding dimensions, with the appearance stream entered as PCA components so it stays comparable. Surrogate fidelity 1.000.

The trap you must not fall into

Most clips in this dataset are labelled "quite engaged". So imagine a lazy guesser that ignores the video completely and always says "quite engaged".

That guesser gets 53% right. It is also completely useless — it has never once looked at a student. If you report accuracy, this useless guesser beats our real model, which gets 47%.

So we use a fairer score called macro-F1, which asks: how well did you do on each of the four levels, counted equally? The lazy guesser scores 0.173 on that. Our model scores 0.286.

Remember this one Never report accuracy on its own for this project. A model that has learned nothing can beat a model that has learned something, and only macro-F1 shows the difference.

What is honestly not working

Three problems, in the order you should care about them.

1 · There is nowhere near enough data

We trained on 36 videos. That is like learning what a dog looks like from 36 photos and then being tested on cats, wolves and foxes. Every number on this page could move a lot with 36 different videos.

Here is the proof it is unstable: the version that scored best on the final test scored worst on the practice test. If the rankings flip depending on which set you look at, the rankings are not measuring anything real yet.

2 · Half of the clever idea does nothing

Remember the "read the fuzzy pages more gently" trick? We measured it. It changes the answer for zero videos.

The reason is almost funny. After we bin the bad pictures at station 1, the ones left over are all quite good — they score between about 8.8 and 9.8 out of 10. So "read gently" versus "read normally" was a difference of about 4%, which the machine simply absorbed and ignored. We aimed the knob at a difference that was not there.

There is a fix already written: instead of comparing each page to a fixed scale, compare it to the other pages in the same video — then the differences are meaningful again. It measures 8× stronger. It is switched off by default so the published results stay reproducible.

3 · The fanciest part is dead weight

When we removed the picture reader entirely, the model got better — from 0.286 up to 0.407. When we removed the ruler instead, it collapsed to 0.052.

Three separate measurements agree: the ablation, the "ask it why" percentages (4.5%), and the judge sitting at 50/50 and never committing. The honest way to say this is not "the picture reader is bad" — it is "the picture reader has not been given enough data to learn from yet." It has 1.1 million dials to set and 36 examples to set them from.

And one thing we never tested at all The whole point of station 1 is throwing away bad pictures. But in this dataset the pictures were nearly all good — out of 5,403 pictures, exactly one was bad enough to bin. So the binning has never actually been put to the test. It needs messier video before we can say whether it helps.

What to do next

  1. Get the full dataset. 9,068 videos instead of 108. The code already handles it — only the folder path changes. This alone addresses problems 1 and 3.
  2. Run each experiment 3–5 times with different random starts, so we can tell a real difference from luck. One-line change.
  3. Switch on the fix for the gentle-reading idea and see whether it earns its place.
  4. Take more pictures per second. At 5 per second we cannot see eye flicks, which last a fraction of that. Some of our eye measurements are therefore not measuring what their names claim.
